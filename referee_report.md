## Summary

The paper turns an institutional accident into an instrument. Because school-entry cutoffs make children born early in the calendar year start first grade at an older age, while compulsory-attendance laws bind until a fixed birthday, first-quarter births reach the legal dropout age with slightly less schooling than later-born peers. The authors document this small but persistent seasonal pattern in educational attainment across cohorts born 1930-1959, show it disappears for post-secondary outcomes that the laws do not reach, and offer difference-in-differences enrollment evidence that the laws hold a minority of would-be dropouts in school. They then use quarter of birth (interacted with year and, later, state of birth) as an instrument for years of schooling in a log-wage equation. The central result is that two-stage least squares returns to education are close to OLS and not significantly different, which the authors read as evidence of little omitted-variables bias in conventional estimates, if anything, OLS is biased slightly downward. The conceptual move, using variation in schooling generated by law and birth timing rather than choice, is what made the paper influential.

The earnings results are robust in the strict sense that they reproduce: an executed replication recovers the Wald and OLS/TSLS tables essentially cell for cell, and follow-up studies extending the design to recent data were also run. The main limitation, clearer in hindsight and confirmed by that follow-up work, is instrument strength. Quarter of birth explains a minute share of schooling, so TSLS is imprecise (t-ratios fall to about 2) and, when instruments are weak, is biased toward OLS, which makes "IV is close to OLS, therefore little bias" a fragile inference rather than a demonstration. A second cluster of issues is internal consistency: a headline dropout fraction that contradicts the abstract, an instrument count at odds with the reported degrees of freedom, and a validity test reported without its statistic.

## Strengths

- **Seed-exact recovery of the return-to-education tables.** The executed replication reproduces Table III's Wald estimates and the eight OLS/TSLS columns of Tables IV, V, and VI to within well under one percent (e.g. Table IV column 2 TSLS 0.0769 with t = 5.1; Panel A Wald 0.0715 against OLS 0.0801), and the exact reported sample sizes, so the core earnings finding rests on values that hold up under re-execution.
- **The post-secondary placebo.** Showing that the seasonal education pattern is present for total schooling and high-school graduation but absent for college, master's, and doctoral completion is a clean internal falsification, and it reproduces: post-secondary quarter-of-birth coefficients are an order of magnitude smaller and lack the systematic first-quarter dip.
- **A control group for the exclusion restriction.** Testing whether quarter of birth predicts earnings among college graduates, whose schooling was not prolonged by the laws, is exactly the right way to probe whether season of birth affects earnings through channels other than compulsory schooling.
- **Cross-state start-age variation to sharpen identification.** Interacting quarter of birth with fifty state-of-birth dummies to exploit differences in school-entry policy is a thoughtful way to add identifying variation and reduce TSLS standard errors.

## Weaknesses

- **The headline "IV close to OLS implies little bias" is weak-instrument-fragile.** Quarter of birth is a very weak predictor of schooling; the reproduced and follow-up diagnostics put the first-stage F for the quarter-by-year instruments around 4.6-6.9, and a weak-IV-robust confidence set for the 1940-1949 cohort is empty. Because weak-IV estimators are biased toward OLS, the coincidence of the TSLS and OLS returns cannot on its own establish the absence of omitted-variables bias, which is the paper's central inferential claim. No first-stage strength statistic or weak-IV-robust interval accompanies any specification.
- **A headline dropout fraction contradicts the abstract.** The body states the 1960 laws kept "approximately one third of potential dropouts in school," which is 4/12 (the difference-in-differences effect over the observed dropout rate), while the abstract says "roughly 25 percent," which is 4/16 (the effect over dropouts plus those retained). The 1980 figure of "roughly 10 percent" follows the 25 percent method, so the "one third" is the inconsistent statement; both numbers cannot come from the same 12 percent and 4 pp inputs.
- **The exclusion restriction leans on an under-reported test.** The college-graduate falsification, which does much of the work of defending quarter of birth as a valid instrument, is reported only as "not rejected at the 25 percent level," with no F statistic, degrees of freedom, exact p-value, or power, and the 1980 result only as "similar." A failure to reject on a small subsample is read as "strong evidence" of no effect, which claims more than the test supports.
- **The instrument count does not match the estimated design.** The text describes three quarters interacted with "nine year-of-birth dummies" (27 instruments), but equation (1) sums over ten birth years and the chi-squared statistics carry 29 over-identifying degrees of freedom, implying 30 instruments; the reproduction used 30. A reader cannot tell how many instruments were actually used.
- **The 1980 enrollment estimate is presented as same-direction support.** The 1964-cohort difference-in-differences effect is near zero (and printed with a sign opposite to the subtraction convention), yet it is grouped with the 1954 estimate as a smaller-but-supporting result rather than flagged as indistinguishable from zero.
- **The "entirely due to compulsory schooling" claim is stronger than the tables.** Several post-secondary F-tests are statistically significant (college graduation; master's and doctoral completion for the 1940s cohort); the authors reasonably ascribe these to sample size, but "entirely" overstates a picture that includes a small residual pattern.

## Reproducibility & code

The executed replication ported the original Stata pipeline to R (linear models and two-stage least squares via standard packages) with no methodological change, and yielded a weighted replication score of 0.74 (18 of 23 claims counted): the earnings claims reproduce strongly, while the enrollment and robustness claims were either not attempted or fell outside the executed scope.

- **Earnings tables reproduce cell for cell.** Tables III, IV, V, and VI and the joint quarter-of-birth F-tests match the paper to within well under one percent at the exact reported sample sizes, so the return-to-education results are faithful to the stated method.
- **The enrollment analysis could not be run.** Table II's difference-in-differences and the derived potential-dropout fractions (about one third in 1960, roughly 10 percent by 1980) require 1960/1970/1980 enrollment samples coded by state legal-dropout age; those inputs and scripts were not available, so these headline enrollment results are unverified. Appendix 1 compounds this by describing the Table II sample as sixteen-year-olds only, omitting the age-fifteen comparison group the estimator needs.
- **Table I matches qualitatively but from a different sample.** First-quarter effects and F-statistics reproduce closely; other cells differ by 5-30 percent because the available extract is the positive-earnings sample rather than the education-based Table I sample that includes non-workers.
- **The declining-education cohorts are absent.** The extract covers birth years 1930-1949 (all rising-education cohorts), so the 1950-1959 panels and the full three-panel Figures III/IV could not be reproduced; within the available years the first-quarter dip appears in 18 of 19 birth years, close to the paper's 27 of 29.
- **State-of-birth and subgroup extensions were not executed.** Table VII's precision gain, the black-men subsample (Table VIII), the weeks-worked variant, and the restricted-education-range check had no runnable scripts and remain unverified.

## Follow-up studies

Two completed follow-up studies extend the design to recent data. A "living update" re-estimates the return over rolling windows: the OLS return to schooling for men 40-49 roughly doubled from 0.063 (1979) to about 0.092-0.100 (2024), while age-16 enrollment is now near-universal (0.972) and the compulsory-schooling enrollment effect has faded to about zero. That study reproduces the paper's 1969/1979 OLS anchors exactly (0.0701/0.0632) and sharpens this report's central weakness: the first-stage F is about 4.6-6.9 and the Anderson-Rubin set for the 1940s cohort is empty, so the instrument is weak in the original data, not only in recent samples. A second study replaces the constant Mincer return with a time-varying skill price; trained on 2005-2014 only, it forecasts the 2015-2024 decline in the return with held-out RMSE 0.0049, against 0.0398 for the original constant-return model, with the relative college supply among men 40-49 rising from 0.234 (1980) to 0.404 (2024). These re-estimates are for the labeled later periods, not the paper's 1970/1980 samples.

## Recommended Changes

**Essential**

- Report first-stage strength (F and partial R-squared) for the quarter-of-birth instruments in each cohort and add weak-IV-robust (Anderson-Rubin) confidence sets, then reinterpret the OLS-TSLS similarity in light of weak-instrument bias toward OLS; this addresses the central weakness in the headline inference.
- Reconcile the "one third of potential dropouts" statement with the abstract's 25 percent so both derive from the same 12 percent dropout rate and 4 pp effect.
- Report the college-graduate falsification test in full (F, degrees of freedom, exact p-value, sample size, and detectable effect) for both 1970 and 1980, since the exclusion restriction rests on it.
- Deposit the 1960/1970/1980 enrollment extracts, the state legal-dropout-age coding, and the difference-in-differences script, and correct Appendix 1 to include the age-fifteen comparison group, so the enrollment effects can be verified.

**Suggested**

- State the instrument count explicitly (30 quarter-by-year interactions) and reconcile it with "nine year-of-birth dummies" and the chi-squared[29] degrees of freedom.
- Report the 1964 enrollment estimate with its sign and note it is indistinguishable from zero rather than grouping it with the 1954 result.
- Qualify "entirely due to compulsory schooling" to reflect the small, sample-size-driven post-secondary F-tests.
- Correct the local numeric and labeling slips: the "0.7 percent" wage gap (the reproduced value is about 0.9 percent, consistent with the 0.072 ratio), the Table VIII "1960 Census" header, the Table II row-6/row-9 labels and the row-9 sign, the Table I citation for the 1920s-versus-1940s comparison, and the individual-index subscripts in equation (1).

## Future Directions

- **Weak-IV-robust re-estimation of the return.** The follow-up's Anderson-Rubin analysis suggests the quarter-of-birth design yields an identified set rather than a point (e.g. roughly [-0.002, 0.178] for the 1930s cohort); reporting such sets, rather than TSLS points, would give an honest characterization of what the instrument can and cannot pin down.
- **Modeling the time-varying skill price.** The second follow-up shows a constant Mincer return cannot fit the post-2010 hump-and-decline; embedding a supply-demand skill price into the original framework is a natural extension of the paper's own attention to secular education trends.
- **Settings where the compulsory margin still binds.** With age-16 enrollment now near-universal in the United States, the instrument is largely spent domestically; applying the age-at-entry/compulsory-schooling design in countries or periods where the dropout margin remains active would recover the identifying variation the original design exploited.
